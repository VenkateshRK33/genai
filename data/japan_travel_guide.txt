Entry requirements and documents

Most short-term visitors need a valid passport with at least six months of remaining validity. Many nationalities can enter Japan without a visa for short tourism stays, but rules differ by country and change over time. Check your exact visa status and any recent entry rule changes on the official Ministry of Foreign Affairs of Japan website or with your nearest Japanese embassy before booking. Keep printed and digital copies of your passport, return ticket, and accommodation bookings.

Customs and arrival

At arrival you will complete immigration and customs checks. There are limits on cash, tobacco, alcohol, and certain goods. Declare anything above the allowed limits and any restricted items honestly. Medication rules are strict; some common prescription and over-the-counter drugs in other countries are restricted or prohibited in Japan, including some inhalers and stimulants. Carry medicines in original packaging with a prescription or a customs certificate (Yunyu Kakunin-sho) if required, and check the rules in advance.

Money, currency, and payments

The currency is the Japanese yen. Japan remains a cash-friendly society; many small restaurants, shrines, and rural places still prefer cash, so carry some yen. Major credit and IC cards are widely accepted in cities. ATMs that accept foreign cards are available at convenience stores such as 7-Eleven and at post offices. Tipping is not customary and can cause confusion; good service is expected as part of the price. Prices are usually shown tax-inclusive in tourist areas, but tax may be added separately in some shops.

Connectivity

Stay connected with a travel SIM, an eSIM, or a pocket Wi-Fi router, which can be picked up at airports or delivered to your hotel. Public free Wi-Fi is available in many cities, stations, and convenience stores but is not always reliable. Download offline maps and translation apps before you travel. Note your embassy contact and emergency numbers offline in case of lost connectivity.

Getting around: trains and IC cards

Japan's rail network is fast, punctual, and extensive. IC contactless cards such as Suica, Pasmo, and ICOCA work on most local trains, subways, buses, and even vending machines and convenience stores. You can load them at stations and recharge as needed. Tap in at the start of a journey and tap out at the end; the fare is calculated automatically.

Shinkansen and long-distance travel

The Shinkansen (bullet train) is the fastest way to travel between major cities such as Tokyo, Kyoto, Osaka, and Hiroshima. Reserve seats in advance during peak periods such as Golden Week, New Year, and summer holidays. Luggage over certain dimensions requires a specific oversized seat reservation, so check size rules before you travel. Compare individual ticket prices against rail passes before buying.

Japan Rail Pass and regional passes

The national Japan Rail Pass covers most JR trains nationwide for a fixed period and suits long, multi-city itineraries, but it may not save money for shorter or city-based trips. Regional passes (for example JR West, JR East, or Kansai area passes) can be better value depending on your route. Always compare pass cost against your planned rides; passes do not cover every private railway or subway line.

Airport transfers

Major gateways include Narita and Haneda for Tokyo, Kansai for Osaka and Kyoto, and Chitose for Hokkaido. Express trains such as the Narita Express, Skyliner, and Haruka connect airports to city centers quickly. Compare the express train, shared shuttle, and bus options by time and price for your specific hotel area.

Etiquette: temples and shrines

At temples and shrines, dress modestly and behave quietly. Follow the cleansing ritual at the fountain (temizuya) before entering a shrine: wash your hands and mouth. Do not walk through the center of a torii gate path; the middle is considered the path of the gods. Photography is not allowed in some halls and gardens, so look for signs.

Etiquette: shoes, onsen, and bathing

Remove your shoes when entering homes, traditional inns (ryokan), temples, and many restaurants; slippers are usually provided. Bathing facilities and hot springs (onsen) require you to wash and rinse thoroughly before entering the bath, and bathing suits are not worn. People with tattoos may be refused entry at some public baths and pools, as tattoos are often associated with organized crime in Japan; check policy in advance.

Etiquette: public behavior

Keep your voice low on trains and buses; phone calls are frowned upon on public transport. Queue in lines marked on platforms and let passengers exit first. Do not eat while walking in most places, and do not tip. Smoking rules are strict, with designated smoking areas; smoking while walking is generally not allowed.

Food and dining

Must-try foods include sushi, ramen, tempura, soba, udon, yakitori, okonomiyaki, and matcha sweets. Convenience stores (konbini) offer surprisingly good, cheap food. Many popular restaurants use ticket machines or require reservations; waits can be long at famous spots. Vegetarian, vegan, and allergy-friendly options exist but are less common, so learn key phrases or carry a dietary card in Japanese.

Seasons and what to pack

Spring (March to May) is mild with cherry blossoms; pack layers and a light jacket. Summer (June to August) is hot and humid; bring breathable clothing, sun protection, and an umbrella for the rainy season. Autumn (September to November) is cool and colorful; layers and a medium jacket work well. Winter (December to February) is cold in much of Japan and snowy in the north; pack a warm coat, and prepare for dry indoor heating.

Packing essentials

Comfortable walking shoes are essential because you will walk a lot. Bring a small day bag, a portable battery, a travel adapter, and basic medicines. Pack for the season, include a reusable umbrella, and keep important documents in a secure, accessible place. Modest clothing is recommended for temple and shrine visits.

Safety and emergencies

Japan is generally very safe, but normal caution still applies. Keep emergency numbers handy: police 110, ambulance and fire 119. Earthquakes happen; know your hotel evacuation route and assembly point. Register your trip with your embassy if possible, and keep digital and printed copies of key documents. Pharmacies are reliable but some familiar medicines are unavailable, so bring essentials.

Tokyo highlights

Tokyo offers neighborhoods for every mood: Shibuya and Shinjuku for neon energy, Asakusa for old-town temples, Ginza for shopping, and Akihabara for electronics and anime. Day trips from Tokyo include Hakone for hot springs and Mt. Fuji views, and Kamakura for temples and the coast. Allow flexible days, as Tokyo rewards wandering.

Kyoto highlights

Kyoto is the cultural heart of Japan with thousands of temples and shrines, including Kinkaku-ji, Fushimi Inari, and Kiyomizu-dera. The Gion district is known for geisha culture and traditional wooden houses. Book popular sights early, and consider renting a bicycle to move between nearby temples. Nara, with its giant Buddha and deer park, is an easy day trip.

Osaka highlights

Osaka is famous for food and friendly energy; try street food in Dotonbori and visit Osaka Castle. It is a convenient base for day trips to Kyoto, Nara, and Kobe. Osaka's transport is well connected and food is generally cheaper than in Tokyo.

Accessibility

Japan is improving accessibility, with more step-free train access and accessible taxis, but some older stations and temples have stairs and uneven paths. Research access in advance for specific needs, and contact your hotel about accessible facilities. Carrying a short note explaining any needs in Japanese can help.

Final reminders

Plan transport and reservations before peak periods, keep cash for small places, and respect local etiquette. Always reconfirm visa, entry, health, transport, and price details with official sources close to your travel date, as rules and schedules change.
